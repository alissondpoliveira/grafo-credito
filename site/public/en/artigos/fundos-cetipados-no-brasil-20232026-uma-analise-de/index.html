Cetipados Funds in Brazil (2023–2026): An Analysis of Infrastructure and Allocation Dynamics

Alisson Prata, CFP® · April 27, 2026

An analysis of the forces that generated 4,604 new vehicles in three years — and what the professional allocator needs to verify before allocating

Technical definition and starting point

A cetipado fund is not an investment category — it is a trading infrastructure category. The term distinguishes closed-end funds whose quotas are registered and traded on B3's organized OTC market (formerly CETIP), as opposed to exchange-listed funds, whose quotas trade in continuous trading sessions with price formation on screen.

This distinction has direct analytical consequences. In an exchange-listed fund, the quota price reflects market consensus at every trade: there is a bid, there is an ask, there is observable volatility. In a cetipado fund, the quota is calculated by the administrator based on the yield curve and the marking of the underlying assets — not on recent secondary market transactions. In the absence of continuous trading, the NAV reported to the quotaholder is an accounting estimate, not a market price. This has two implications that run through this entire text: the first is behavioral (the quotaholder sees no swings on the statement because there is no continuous price formation); the second is about risk (the absence of an observable price does not mean the absence of credit or market risk — it means that risk is deferred until the next forced pricing event).

With that technical foundation established: between January 2023 and April 2026, the number of new closed-end fund classes registered with CVM grew from 330 to more than 2,200 per year. What explains this growth, what it represents in terms of real risk for the quotaholder, and how a professional allocator should filter this universe are the three questions this text examines.

The mechanism that generated the boom

Every financial innovation cycle begins with a real problem and ends with product excess. The cetipados cycle began with two simultaneous problems, both real, both verifiable in the data.

The first problem was on the demand side. On January 2, 2023, the mandatory mark-to-market requirement for private fixed-income securities took effect in client portfolios of institutions bound by ANBIMA's Distribution Code: debentures, CRIs and CRAs began to be reported at updated market value, no longer at curve value. That same week, Americanas disclosed the accounting inconsistencies that triggered its judicial reorganization. According to an Itaú BBA estimate reported by Bloomberg Línea in March 2023, all funds with at least 15% of the portfolio allocated to private credit — a universe that includes multimarket funds, fixed-income funds and credit funds of every category — accumulated R$ 66 billion in net redemptions between January 12 and March 2 of that year. In the specific category of funds classified as private credit, ANBIMA recorded R$ 5.1 billion in redemptions in January and R$ 12.2 billion in February — R$ 17.3 billion for the period. The difference between the two figures reflects the scope of the universes measured: the R$ 66 billion impact captures credit exposure distributed across all fund categories, not only those nominally classified as such.

The combined effect was direct: the investor who had never seen volatility on the statement began to see swings of 2%, 3%, 5% in products considered conservative. Demand for instruments that would not display such variation became concrete. The closed-end fund solves this directly: the quota does not mark to market at the frequency the investor checks it, and the fixed term creates the perception of a controlled horizon.

The second problem was on the supply side. CVM Resolution 160, which governs public offerings of closed-end fund quotas, and CVM Resolution 175, in force since October 2023, standardized class registration and reduced the operating cost of creating and distributing cetipados. The regulatory infrastructure to launch these funds at scale came into existence precisely when behavioral demand existed to absorb them.

Two simultaneous problems, one solution in the same regulatory format. Understanding this overlap is what allows the growth to be assessed beyond the product narrative.

What the CVM data reveals

Between January 2023 and April 2026, 4,604 new closed-end fund classes were registered in the FII, FIP, FIDC and FIAGRO categories — the cetipável universe — in the registro_classe.csv and registro_fundo.csv datasets made available by CVM as open data. A methodological note is necessary: registro_classe.csv counts classes and subclasses, not unique CNPJs. RCVM 175 introduced the umbrella fund structure, in which a single CNPJ can concentrate multiple classes with distinct strategies and target audiences. A portion of the growth recorded in 2024 and 2025 reflects the reorganization of existing structures into new classes to comply with the new regulatory framework, in addition to the creation of genuinely new vehicles. CVM's open data does not allow this difference to be isolated directly, so the number of new CNPJs is lower than the number of registered classes. Real growth is significant under any counting methodology. The year-by-year evolution is direct:

2023: 330 new closed-end classes. 2024: 1,877, up 469% from the prior year. 2025: 2,299, an additional increase of 22%. 2026 (January to April): 98.

The acceleration from 330 to more than 2,200 new classes per year between 2023 and 2025 does not find proportional explanation in verifiable demand variation. The Selic rate went from 13.75% per year at the start of 2023 through a cycle that brought it to 10.5% by mid-2024, before a new upward move (BCB, COPOM history). High interest rates encourage the search for post-fixed credit instruments with a premium over CDI — which explains the direction of growth, but not the scale. The data point that would establish this relationship rigorously — the evolution of the average number of quotaholders per fund or average NAV per class over the years — is not available in CVM's open data in a format that allows direct isolation. What the data does allow us to state is that the growth in the number of vehicles has no equivalent correlate in aggregate fundraising per vehicle: total FIDC fundraising reached R$ 81.4 billion in 2024 (ANBIMA), while the number of new FIDCs grew 578% between 2023 and 2024 — suggesting fragmentation of the asset base across multiple vehicles, not proportional expansion of the investor base. The regulatory window, distribution incentives and the reorganization effect of existing structures under RCVM 175 account for a relevant part of the delta.

The composition by fund type reveals a relevant rotation. In 2024, FIDCs accounted for 1,218 of the 1,877 new closed-end funds, 65% of the flow, and the category's fundraising reached R$ 81.4 billion for the year, up 86.1% from 2023, according to ANBIMA. In 2025, FIDCs fell to 246 new classes, while FIPs rose to 1,157 and FIIs to 789: FIPs and FIIs went from 26% to 85% of the new classes registered in the year. This rotation coincides with the moment when net spreads on cetipado FIDCs proved lower than qualified investor expectations, and the flow of new registrations migrated toward structures with longer return horizons and less comparability with short-term benchmarks.

Administrative concentration confirms the supply-side logic. CVM's open data reveals that the largest administrator in the cetipado universe during the period figures in more than 640 of the 4,604 vehicles, 14% of the universe, with combined net assets exceeding R$ 240 billion. The three largest administrators combined account for more than 60% of total NAV. This degree of concentration reflects the scale advantage of consolidated platforms, which reduce the marginal cost of launching new vehicles. The data is derived from matching by administrator CNPJ in CVM's open data, excluding funds with NAV above R$ 50 billion.

The four forces sustaining the growth

The cetipados boom does not have one cause. It has four, operating on distinct layers with independent logics.

The first is behavioral, on the demand side. The mandatory mark-to-market requirement in effect since January 2023 and the Americanas episode created aversion to visible volatility. Not to real risk, but to the representation of risk on the statement. The closed-end fund solves this: the quota does not swing at the frequency the investor checks it, and illiquidity is framed as a feature, not a constraint. For high-net-worth investor profiles with long-term allocation horizons, this framing makes genuine sense.

The second force is regulatory and economic, on the supply side. RCVM 160 and RCVM 175 reduced the cost and time to create and distribute closed-end funds by standardizing class registration and public offering documents. From the supply side, the closed-end fund has a distribution compensation structure different from the open-end fund: the distribution fee is recognized when fundraising closes, whereas in the open-end fund the equivalent compensation tends to be diluted over the product's life cycle. This structural difference is a fact the quotaholder should incorporate when understanding the product being acquired.

The third force is structural and operates in corporate credit. With the Selic rate held above 10% per year since 2021 (BCB, COPOM history), mid-sized companies began seeking alternatives to bank credit. The FIDC allows receivables to be securitized directly in the capital markets, potentially at a lower cost than intermediated bank credit, without depending on a bank's balance sheet. The growth of aggregate FIDC NAV, which reached R$ 741.1 billion in the twelve months ended November 2025, up 22.5% in the period, according to ANBIMA data from December 2025, reflects this real demand for credit intermediation outside the banking system. The problem is not the instrument. It is that the same wrapper serves both to securitize receivables of a company with solid cash flow and to structure lower-quality assets with the appearance of structured credit.

The fourth force is fiscal, with a mechanism more specific than the usual formulation suggests. Law 14,754, enacted in December 2023, instituted semiannual withholding tax (come-cotas) on conventional closed-end investment funds — closed-end Multimarket Funds and Fixed Income Funds — eliminating the tax deferral these vehicles provided. The most direct impact was not on the retail investor acquiring FII quotas over the counter. It was on the wealth manager who structured closed-end exclusive and super-exclusive funds as accumulation vehicles with tax deferral for high-net-worth clients: these funds accumulated returns without periodic come-cotas; after the law, that deferral ended. Cetipado FIIs, Fiagros and FIPs became structural substitutes — they preserve the come-cotas exemption for individuals, which was never withdrawn from them. This is the more precise migration mechanism: not new investor demand for cetipados, but a reorganization of wealth governance structures toward vehicles with tax treatment equivalent to the previous one. The same law taxed offshore funds at a 15% rate starting in 2025. The claim that "repatriated offshore capital migrated to domestic cetipados" is plausible as a hypothesis; it is not documented in flow data available for this text and should be read as inference, not established fact.

What a representative sample reveals about the asset class

To the investor arriving at the segment by name alone, the cetipado universe appears homogeneous: closed-end funds, fixed term, no redemption window. Analysis of a sample of 43 active funds with confirmed distribution, covering FIIs, Fiagros, infrastructure funds and private credit structures, reveals the opposite. It is not one asset class. It is a spectrum ranging from senior secured credit with explicit subordination to pure equity with no return floor.

The spectrum of benchmarks

A cetipado fund's benchmark signals the nature of the risk assumed and the return horizon of the strategy. In the sample analyzed, four benchmark families coexist within the same regulatory universe — and the same fund category can operate with different benchmarks depending on the manager's strategy and the profile of the underlying assets.

The first family is CDI with a fixed spread. The spread observed in the sample ranges from 1.5 to 4.5 percentage points per year, with the floor associated with structures with greater protection — high subordination or solid real guarantees — and the ceiling present in strategies with greater relative risk, such as credit concentration or assets without external ratings. A relevant point for the individual investor: a significant portion of the cetipado universe is exempt from individual income tax on returns, which significantly raises net return relative to taxed instruments with an equivalent benchmark.

The second family is IPCA with a fixed spread. The range observed runs from IPCA + 4% to IPCA + 8%, with the ceiling concentrated in structures with greater perceived regulatory or execution risk. This benchmark implies long duration — the investor maintains exposure to the inflation index for the fund's entire term. The individual income tax exemption, where applicable, significantly amplifies net return in this format.

The third family is the fixed rate. A fixed return is viable in structures where the average turnover period of underlying assets is much shorter than the fund's term, allowing the manager to adjust portfolio yield without accumulating duration risk relevant to the quotaholder.

The fourth family is not a fixed-income benchmark: it is an IRR target. Funds that invest in project or company equity declare a target internal rate of return in their regulations, typically between 15% and 25% per year in nominal terms. This is the only format where the investor assumes pure equity risk — no return floor, no priority in the waterfall, no guarantee of principal return.

Risk architecture: waterfall, subordination and guarantees

The second dimension of diversity is the capital protection structure. Within the same regulatory universe coexist funds with multiple layers of protection and funds where the quotaholder is entirely exposed to the portfolio's residual risk.

In FIDCs, the most common structure is the division between senior and subordinated quotas. The senior has priority in cash distribution and principal return; the subordinated absorbs losses first, functioning as a protective cushion. The subordination ratio — the proportion of NAV held in subordinated quotas — is the main indicator of effective protection. Funds with 20% subordination offer radically different protection from funds with 5%, even if they share the same benchmark and stated term. More elaborate structures add a mezzanine tranche between the two extremes, creating a three-tier loss-absorption waterfall.

In cetipado receivables FIIs, protection comes from a different mechanism: a real guarantee on the properties backing the CRIs in the portfolio. The relevant question here is not about subordination, but about LTV — the ratio between the outstanding balance of the receivables and the updated value of the property given as collateral. A fund with an average LTV of 55% carries a significantly different protection cushion from a fund with an LTV of 85%, even if both present themselves as real estate receivables funds with a target return of IPCA + 6%.

In agricultural credit Fiagros, guarantees vary among crop pledges, fiduciary liens on rural property, producer guarantees and fiduciary assignment of export receivables. The quality and liquidity of these guarantees vary significantly by crop, by region and by commodity price cycle. Credit backed by exported soybeans has a different execution profile than credit backed by sugarcane crops destined for the domestic market.

In FIPs, there is no structural guarantee: the quotaholder buys stakes in companies or projects, and the return depends entirely on asset performance. Protection, where it exists, comes from contractual exit-preference clauses or ratchet mechanisms, not from subordination or real collateral. It is the format with the greatest information asymmetry between manager and quotaholder.

The sectors where capital is allocated

The third dimension of diversity is the destination of the capital. Mapping the sample by allocation sector, five blocks emerge with distinct characteristics of risk, cycle and underlying asset liquidity.

Logistics and corporate real estate: cetipado FIIs that acquire properties directly or finance their construction and acquisition via CRI. Return is composed of rental yield (entry cap rate) and capital gain upon sale at the end of the term. The main risk is vacancy and the real estate cycle. Asset liquidity is sufficient for guarantee execution within reasonable timeframes, which distinguishes this segment from others with less fungible collateral.

Agricultural credit: Fiagros that finance rural producers via CRA or directly structured operations. Return is directly linked to the commodity cycle, sector delinquency and the effectiveness of safeguards during periods of stress. The exceptional returns observed in 2025 in part of this sample reflect asset repricing following a delinquency cycle in 2024, not structural acceleration in profitability, and should not be extrapolated as a current return expectation for the segment.

Infrastructure: FIP-IE and FI-Infra funds that invest in debentures of solar and wind energy projects, transmission, basic sanitation and road concessions. Return is indexed to IPCA with a fixed spread and structured in predictable flows under concession contracts, provided the project does not face regulatory or execution risk. This segment concentrates the sample's longest duration and the greatest sensitivity to sharp moves in the real yield curve.

Corporate credit: FIDCs that acquire receivables from mid-sized companies, trade invoices and working capital operations. Risk is credit concentrated in the originator and in the quality of the receivables portfolio. The subordination structure is the main protection mechanism, and its effectiveness depends on the subordination ratio, portfolio diversification and the quality of the manager's origination policy.

Individual credit: payroll-deduction FIDCs, vehicle financing and unsecured personal credit. Risk is spread across thousands of individual debtors, which reduces specific concentration but exposes the fund to macroeconomic employment and income cycles systemically. Delinquency recovery rates vary significantly by modality: public-sector payroll credit has recovery close to 100% due to the payroll-deduction mechanism; unsecured personal credit, much lower.

What the diversity implies

Two cetipado funds with the same five-year term, the same classification as a limited-liability closed-end fund and the same classification on the distribution platform can have radically different risk profiles: a senior payroll-deduction FIDC with 20% subordination and an equity FIP in agro-industrial projects. The regulatory wrapper is identical. The content has no dimension in common.

This heterogeneity is not a flaw in the segment. It is its defining characteristic. And it is the strongest argument against any evaluation based on category: the product's name does not inform the product's risk.

Universe segmentation and structural risk map

The number 4,604 needs to be dissected before any inference. Treating it as a homogeneous mass is the first analytical error.

Two distinct groups make up this universe, with entirely different logics and risks.

Shelf products: vehicles designed for platform distribution, with public offerings and fundraising directed at the high-net-worth retail segment and qualified investors. It is in this group that oversupply risk resides — proliferation of vehicles without proportional growth in the investor base, and without manager selection keeping pace with the rate of launches. The growth from 330 to more than 2,200 new classes per year is, to a large extent, the story of this group.

Governance vehicles: structures created by Single-Family Offices, Private Banks and wealth managers to consolidate capital and optimize taxation following Law 14,754/2023. Family-participation FIPs, single-property FIIs, FIDCs holding receivables of the same economic group. These are not distribution products — they are infrastructural efficiency migrations. The same wealth, repackaged in cetipado format for tax or operational reasons. The risk here is not inadequate distribution. It is governance and intra-group asset pricing. Those who migrated for tax reasons know the vehicle they are using. Those who arrived through the shelf product generally know only the statement.

CVM's open data confirms this divide: dozens of funds with NAV above R$ 500 million and fewer than 5 quotaholders are part of the universe of 4,604. Without separating these two groups, the number does not precisely support any argument.

Opacity risk from curve marking. This is the most relevant risk for the retail quotaholder and the least visible until it materializes. Under normal conditions, the absence of continuous pricing in the secondary market produces the apparent NAV stability that drove the behavioral demand described earlier. Under credit stress conditions, this same absence means the deterioration of underlying assets is not visible on the statement until the moment the administrator is forced to mark to market — due to declared delinquency, restructuring or guarantee execution. The result is the abrupt markdown documented in the 2024 agro delinquency cycle, in which funds with stable NAV recorded drops of 10% to 20% in isolated months. Technically, this is not volatility — it is the collapse of the lag between accounting NAV and the recoverable value of the assets. The secondary market for cetipado quotas amplifies this effect: thin under normal conditions, it can become nonexistent under stress, forcing the quotaholder who needs to exit before maturity to accept significant discounts on the reported NAV. The apparent stability of the cetipado quota is not a property of the asset. It is a property of the pricing methodology.

Platform concentration risk. When a single administrator manages more than 600 closed-end funds for an overlapping client base, a stress event at the administrator — regulatory, operational or reputational — affects formally independent positions simultaneously. This risk does not appear in any individual fund's prospectus and is only measurable at the aggregate level.

Sizing risk. According to CVM's open data, 17.2% of closed-end funds registered in the period have NAV below R$ 10 million. For a FIDC or FIP in that range, fixed structural costs — administration, custody, oversight, audit — consume a relevant portion of gross return before any illiquidity premium is calculated. The net return to quotaholders of these funds, in general, does not compensate for the illiquidity risk assumed.

Allocation criteria: what the professional allocator must verify

The analysis above does not condemn the product. It identifies the conditions under which it is suitable.

There is a methodological warning that precedes any checklist: in cetipado funds, retrospective performance analysis is not due diligence — it is noise with the appearance of signal. Curve marking produces artificially stable historical NAV series. They do not reveal the quality of the underlying portfolio. The monthly statement does not tell the story of risk — it tells the accounting story. These two stories diverge silently until a credit event forces them to converge abruptly.

Due diligence needs to be prospective. The allocator does not analyze what the fund has delivered — it analyzes how the manager originates, selects and monitors assets before any credit event. The questions that distribution materials do not answer:

Liquidity of the overall portfolio. Capital allocated to cetipados should represent a defined, sustainable fraction of real illiquidity in the investor's total portfolio. The question is not "what is the fund's term," but "what is the impact on portfolio liquidity if that capital is unavailable for the maximum stated term."

Verifiable net premium. The premium over the equivalent liquid instrument, calculated after all structure fees — administration, performance, distribution and custody — should be positive and proportional to the risk assumed. In funds with NAV below R$ 100 million, fixed structural costs systematically compress this premium. The calculation should be made, not assumed.

Track record in the specific strategy. The difference between a manager with an auditable track record in the proposed strategy and a manager with two years of existence and growing NAV is information about portfolio behavior in adverse cycles. Fundraising history does not substitute for performance history.

Pricing transparency. In cetipado funds, apparent NAV stability is not evidence of low risk — it is evidence of curve-marking methodology. The allocator should verify: how often and under what conditions does the administrator mark assets to market? How did NAV behave during periods of stress in the segment? Is there a functional secondary market for the quotas, and at what discount?

Manager alignment after fundraising. The quotaholder should verify how the strategy manager maintains exposure to the fund's performance after fundraising closes: direct stake in the vehicle, performance fee calculated on actual return — not on projected IRR — and any lock-up mechanisms applicable to the manager itself. The history of closures and liquidations of the same manager's prior funds is a relevant source of information about this behavior over the cycle.

Summary

The growth of 4,604 new cetipado fund classes between 2023 and 2026 had four converging forces: behavioral demand for instruments without visible volatility on the statement; reduced regulatory cost of creating closed-end vehicles under RCVM 175; an incentive structure in the distribution chain favoring revenue recognition at fundraising; and the elimination of tax deferral in conventional exclusive funds under Law 14,754/2023, which directed the reorganization of high-net-worth wealth toward cetipado structures exempt from come-cotas.

Of this universe, a relevant fraction is made up of wealth governance vehicles and institutional structures, not distribution products for the retail qualified investor. The same regulatory wrapper houses everything from senior FIDCs with documented subordination and auditable portfolios to equity FIPs with no structural guarantee and undersized funds where fixed costs erode the illiquidity premium. The category does not inform the risk.

The illiquidity premium exists. In well-sized structures, with verifiable origination and documented incentive alignment, it is capturable. The problem is not the instrument — it is the selection.

The monthly statement of a cetipado fund does not measure risk. It defers it. The absence of visible volatility is not a property of the asset — it is a property of the pricing method. As long as credit does not deteriorate to the point of forcing a mark to market, the statement will always be correct and never informative.

Selection rigor is the only real mark to market available to the allocator. Confusing statement stability with asset quality is the mistake this market will still collect from many portfolios.
