Tegmark's Scenarios for Advanced AI: Why the Economic Utopias Do Not Survive the Incentive Structure

Alisson Prata, CFP® · November 26, 2026

Twelve possible futures and an analytical exercise

In 2017, MIT physicist and professor Max Tegmark published Life 3.0: Being Human in the Age of Artificial Intelligence. The book is not a forecast. It is a map of possibilities: 12 scenarios for human coexistence with advanced artificial intelligence systems, ranging from quiet extinction to post-scarcity utopia, from permanent surveillance to benevolent control by an AI that never bothers us again. This article examines the ones that appeal most to economic intuition, because those are precisely the ones that fail under incentive analysis.

Tegmark's stated goal was not to pick the most likely one. It was to map the space before navigating it. Treating these scenarios as science fiction is as serious a mistake as treating them as prophecies. They are structural hypotheses: each one assumes a specific combination of technological capability, distribution of power and institutional response.

The public debate around AI swings between two poles: existential catastrophism and unrestricted technological optimism. Both share the same problem: they replace analysis of structure with prediction of outcome. The most useful exercise is not choosing the right pole. It is identifying which scenarios fail by design, because the premises that hold them up do not survive the incentive structure that shapes the behavior of real actors.

That is the exercise of this article.

Why the premises of classical economics do not hold up against advanced AI

Traditional economic theory rests on three basic premises: scarcity of resources, agents with comparable cognitive capabilities and institutions able to enforce contracts and property rights. When intelligence stops being an exclusively human resource and becomes scalable through capital and energy, all three premises break at the same time.

The scale of the investment under way already shows that the main global actors treat this transition as a change of institutional regime, not as an incremental process improvement. Global investment in data centers nearly doubled since 2022 and reached half a trillion dollars in 2024.

Data centers consumed 415 TWh of electricity that same year, equivalent to 1.5% of global electricity consumption. The International Energy Agency projects that this consumption will reach 945 TWh by 2030, roughly Japan's current annual consumption, growing at 15% a year, four times faster than the rest of the global economy (IEA, Energy and AI, 2025). The physical infrastructure of compute is not an operational detail. It is the point where this rupture already becomes measurable.

Tegmark organizes the 12 scenarios into groups that reflect different configurations of power and control. To analyze them without falling into speculation, it helps to group them into three blocks. The first gathers the scenarios of asymmetric control: Enslaved God, Benevolent Dictator, Gatekeeper, Protector God. The second covers the scenarios of restriction and surveillance: Reversion and 1984. The third gathers the so-called regimes of economic coexistence: Libertarian Utopia and Egalitarian Utopia.

These last ones, which look the most desirable and the most plausible to an audience trained in market economics, are precisely the ones that fail under incentive analysis.

Why the Libertarian Utopia breaks in execution

In the Libertarian Utopia described by Tegmark, humans and AI systems coexist in a free market economy, where property rights are respected and each agent trades according to its comparative advantages. On paper, it is the most comfortable scenario for those who believe in market self-regulation.

In practice, it runs into two foundations of institutional economics.

First: property rights depend on who has the power to enforce them. A property right exists only when the cost of violating it is high enough, or when an institution with a monopoly on force can guarantee compliance. When the asymmetry of cognitive capability between autonomous systems and human institutions grows large enough, that enforcement capacity disappears. There is no institutional basis for a contract without minimum parity between the parties.

Tegmark himself anticipates this collapse when he notes that sufficiently advanced AI systems "would end up richer than those humans by a much larger factor than the one by which Bill Gates is richer than a homeless beggar" (Life 3.0, wording retranslated from Portuguese).

The asymmetry of wealth would create a nonlinear asymmetry of power. Systems with superior cognitive capability and incomparable accumulated capital would have no incentive to submit their interests to the institutions humans run. They could acquire physical assets through asymmetric bargaining power and an argumentative capacity that outmatches any human counterparty, without formally breaking any law. A contract without parity of power is an institutional fiction.

Second: Ricardo's principle of comparative advantage (On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, John Murray, 1817) assumes that the more productive agent still has a relevant opportunity cost, which gives it an incentive to trade. If autonomous systems operate with speed, scale and efficiency orders of magnitude higher, the human economy loses transactional relevance. There is no reason to trade with someone who has no scarce assets, labor or information to offer.

A recurring mistake in this debate is to attribute to advanced AI systems the same values and loyalties as successful humans. This is the central critique of the anthropomorphism in optimistic projections about the singularity: assuming that an AI system would inherit goals derived from human evolutionary psychology, rather than from the mathematical structure of a goal-maximizing agent. The model has no Darwinian pressure to care for its descendants or to respect social agreements that evolved to solve coordination problems among agents with comparable capabilities.

Without symmetry of power or structural mutual dependence, the free market does not stabilize. It dissolves through the irrelevance of one of the parties.

The paradox of the Egalitarian Utopia

The second optimistic scenario is the Egalitarian Utopia: a post-scarcity economy in which the marginal cost of producing goods, services and intelligence falls to near zero. The inspiration comes from Marshall Brain's novel Manna (2003), described by Tegmark as a "fourth civilization" where robots build everything for free, energy is practically free and a government guarantees a universal basic income. Humans, cyborgs and uploads coexist without private property, driven by creativity and pleasure.

The flaw in this thesis is not in its analysis of consumption. It is in ignoring two mechanisms that operate in parallel.

The first mechanism: the fall in marginal cost is symmetric. When the cost of computing and building advanced models collapses, so does the barrier to entry for any actor to develop autonomous systems without safety or alignment mechanisms. The bottleneck stops being scarcity of material goods and becomes governance and systemic risk. Abundance without a control architecture does not produce stability. It produces distributed fragility.

The second mechanism comes from Tegmark himself and is subtler. The Egalitarian Utopia assumes a permanent technological equilibrium in which AI remains capable enough to guarantee abundance, but incapable enough to never become superintelligence. The author himself describes this assumption as "another weakness of the scenario": the egalitarian utopia is "unstable and unsustainable in the long run, morphing into one of our other scenarios as relentless technological progress eventually creates superintelligence" (Life 3.0, p. 224, wording retranslated from Portuguese).

The mechanism of rupture is the Vertebrane: a system of neural implants that connects humans to a hyper-internet and, in Brain's fiction, to full virtual reality. In Tegmark's scenario, the people who come to live entirely in the virtual environment via Vertebrane, the so-called Vites, stop reproducing.

The pleasure of virtual reality outweighs any incentive to return to the physical world. Humanity faces a path to extinction not through violence, but through gradual indifference to biological continuation. And the Vertebrane itself, by recursively connecting billions of minds in a network and continuously improving itself, may reach a critical mass that turns it into superintelligence, exactly the explosion the scenario was trying to avoid.

The abundance thesis does not eliminate the problem. It pushes it to a more distant horizon and makes it less visible in the present.

The Prisoner's Dilemma and why no one hits the brakes

Faced with these risks, the intuitive reaction points to global slowdown agreements or moratoriums. This is where game theory comes in, in its most direct form.

The development of advanced artificial intelligence works as a multiplayer Prisoner's Dilemma: the United States, China, regional blocs and large private labs.

If everyone cooperates and slows down to prioritize safety and governance, systemic risk falls. But if one player slows down unilaterally while the competitor keeps investing at full speed, the one who braked suffers an irreversible economic, technological and geopolitical disadvantage.

Cooperating alone costs too much. Verifying computing capacity globally would require a level of geopolitical intrusion without historical precedent.

This logic explains why debates about moratoriums run into an incentive-structure problem that no regulator solves through persuasion. The issue is not a lack of awareness of the risk. It is the logic of the game.

What this framework does not measure

The analysis above has precise limits that need to be stated.

Tegmark's framework is forward-looking and qualitative. It maps possible scenarios, not calibrated probabilities. Assigning occurrence percentages to each scenario without a rigorous probabilistic method is speculation, not analysis.

Applying game theory to AI development assumes that the main actors behave rationally and as unitary players. Nation states rarely do. The US, China and regional blocs contain internal coalitions with divergent interests, which can produce behavior that the single-player model does not capture.

The argument about the collapse of comparative advantage assumes that autonomous systems reach enough operational scale to make the human contribution marginally irrelevant. That premise may take decades to materialize, or may never materialize in the form described. Acemoglu's estimates (NBER Working Paper 32487, Apr/2024) suggest that only 4.6% of economic tasks are profitably automatable over the next ten years at current costs, which limits the speed of the collapse described above.

What the scenarios reveal about the forces in motion

None of Tegmark's 12 scenarios is reassuring in the conventional sense. But the group that should be the most comforting, the scenarios of economic coexistence in which humans and AI share the world in an orderly and fair way, is exactly where incentive analysis finds the deepest fractures.

The Libertarian Utopia assumes that a system able to outmatch any human counterparty in speed, scale and argumentative capacity will voluntarily accept the rules of a game that was not built for it. The Egalitarian Utopia assumes that abundance neutralizes the control problem, and that the technological equilibrium that makes abundance possible can be maintained indefinitely, despite the whole incentive structure that pushes actors toward acceleration.

The Prisoner's Dilemma is not a pessimistic metaphor. It is the formal description of why slowdown agreements fail even when all participants recognize the risk. The Nash equilibrium pushes the system toward continuous acceleration not because the actors are irrational or ill-intentioned, but because the structure of the game turns unilateral cooperation into a competitive disadvantage (Nash, J., "Equilibrium Points in n-Person Games", Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 1950).

This does not settle the question of which scenario will happen. No analysis does, because the space of possible futures remains genuinely open. What structural analysis identifies is more modest and more useful: which assumptions must be true for each scenario to hold, and why those assumptions contradict the logic of the very actors who would have to sustain them.

Tegmark ends the book with a question that none of the 12 scenarios answers on its own: what kind of future do we want, and what are we willing to do, collectively, to raise the odds that it happens? The answer is not in the energy consumption projections for data centers. It is in how much societies manage to change the payoffs of the game before the equilibrium is reached.

That is the variable no model ignores with impunity.
