The Discount Rate Governed the First Half of 2026

Alisson Prata, CFP® · July 21, 2026

One principle explains the American rally, the Brazilian correction, the long bond massacre, and Bitcoin's decline.

By the cultural yardstick of the Brazilian investor, a good half-year is one in which the stock market rises. In the first half of 2026, the stock market rose. The Ibovespa advanced 6.76%, the S&P 500 hit new highs, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index doubled in value.

The CDI yielded 6.90% over the same period.

With the CDI at this level, the ranking flips. Whoever held the Ibovespa saw the index rise 23% through mid-April, give back 13% by the end of June, and finish behind those who accepted no volatility at all. The premium for taking equity risk in Brazil was, in realized terms, negative. And this was not a local accident. It was the Brazilian manifestation of a principle that governed the entire half-year, across every geography and every asset class.

The Half-Year Rewarded Present Cash and Punished Promise

The principle is simple. When the real interest rate is high, the discount rate becomes the dominant factor in price formation. Every year of waiting costs dearly, and the present value of a distant cash flow shrinks more than that of a near one. The practical consequence: assets that deliver cash now beat assets that promise cash later, unless the promise grows fast enough to offset the larger discount. That exception decides the top of the ranking.

The mechanism explains the hierarchy of returns, and its top is reached by two distinct paths. One is the mechanical carry of high interest rates: CDI-indexed debentures yielded 7.09% and the Tesouro Selic, 6.95%, simply by capturing the Selic rate at the level it was at. The other is the exception of the promise that comes true: semiconductors led the half-year, driven by memory makers, not because they have short duration, but because earnings revisions, anchored in results already delivered, ran faster than the rise in the cost of capital. Their high multiple embeds future growth, like every high multiple, but it was growth that earnings releases kept confirming quarter after quarter. They beat the gravity of the discount through the numerator, not by escaping it. In the middle, the conditional promise: the Ibovespa, which depended on falling rates to unlock its discount and got neither a sufficient cut nor an earnings shock, delivered 6.76% with pain. At the bottom, pure promise: the longest Brazilian government bond lost about 15% in June alone, and Bitcoin, which promises no cash flow at any date whatsoever, fell 33%.

The pattern is not new. The year 2022 in the United States ran the same movie, with rates rising, unprofitable tech melting down, and energy paying off. The difference in 2026 is that Brazil lived both ends at once: a rally bought from abroad and a discount rate that never stopped charging its toll at home.

The Brazilian Stock Market Rose in Two Acts and Lost on the Final Scoreboard

The first act lasted until April 14. Driven by the largest foreign capital inflow for a start of the year since 2022, with R$ 26.3 billion in January alone, the Ibovespa climbed from 161,125 points at the end of 2025 to a closing record of 198,657 points, a gain of 23.3% in a little over three months. The accumulated foreign net flow for the year neared R$ 67.7 billion by mid-April, and foreign investors accounted for 61% of B3's trading volume.

The second act began right there. Between the record and the end of June, the index gave back 13.4%, with an 8.24% decline concentrated in the second quarter. Foreign investors withdrew R$ 14.9 billion in May and another R$ 8.8 billion in June, and the half-year net flow in the secondary market closed at R$ 33.8 billion. The flow that manufactured the rally also manufactured the correction.

Meanwhile, short fixed income was doing the quiet work. The CDI accumulated 6.90%, and only two ANBIMA indices beat that mark: CDI-indexed debentures, at 7.09%, and the Tesouro Selic, at 6.95%. At the long end, the massacre. The IMA-B 5+, which tracks the longest inflation-linked bonds, yielded 2.20%, less than a third of the CDI, with the ten-year real rate nearing IPCA plus 7.5% and the Tesouro IPCA+ 2035 paying as much as 8.10% in real terms in June.

The American Rally Had Earnings. The Brazilian Rally Had Flow

Here lies the half-year's central argument, and it requires looking at both markets at once.

In the United States, the S&P 500 rose 10.2% backed by earnings. Consensus expected 13.1% growth for first-quarter results; the actual figure came in near 28%, with 84% of companies beating estimates, and the 2026 earnings growth projection was revised from 13.6% at the start of the year to 23.3% by the end of June. An honest caveat: part of the surprise came from non-operating accounting items at three tech giants, and broad-based growth, excluding these effects, came in near 14%. Even so, the direction is unambiguous. Analysts spent the half-year revising American earnings upward.

In Brazil, the movement was the opposite. Cumulative revisions to the Ibovespa's projected 2026 earnings added up to an 11.5% decline through April, with expected full-level growth cut to 5.8%, dragged down by commodities and by the real's appreciation. The Brazilian rally of the first quarter did not buy expanding earnings. It bought a discount, and a qualification is in order here. The index traded at 9 to 10 times earnings, below the historical average of 10.5, but this aggregate multiple is skewed downward by the weight of Vale and Petrobras at a commodity cycle peak, which inflates the denominator's earnings. Excluding the two, the multiple rises to the 11-to-12 times range, at or above the historical average. The apparent price was flattened by top-of-cycle commodity accounting. The real price, with the distortion removed, was no bargain. By this very text's yardstick, it was worse: paying 11 to 12 times earnings with the ten-year real rate at IPCA plus 7.5% narrows the equity premium over real fixed income to a thin band. It was not a discount, it was a thin premium. The rally did not buy cheap fundamentals. It bought the bet, financed by foreign flow, that falling rates would restore the premium that high rates had compressed.

Fact and inference need to be kept separate at this point. The fact is the asymmetry of revisions: American earnings up, Brazilian earnings down. The inference is mine: the B3 correction was less a judgment on Brazil and more the local effect of a global risk repricing, triggered when earnings showed up on one side of the map and not the other. The domestic deterioration in inflation and rates helped, but the trigger was external.

It's worth not tracing where that capital went, because the data do not support any clean destination. It did not go back to the United States: during the half-year, equity flow left there, with non-US equity ETFs accounting for 34% of inflows, against a market share of 20%. Nor did it migrate to Asian tech emerging markets, the most intuitive hypothesis after the strong performance of Korea and Taiwan in the first months of the year: in June, these two markets were the epicenter of the emerging-market equity sell-off, not its destination, with US$30.5 billion pulled out of South Korea, the largest foreign outflow in more than 25 years, and US$18.3 billion from Taiwan. It wasn't rotation from one market to another. It was the same factor compressing everything at once. The IIF attributed the June outflow to higher global discount rates and sensitivity to positioning in technology. It is this text's yardstick on a global scale: when the discount rises, the long promise loses in any time zone.

The engine of this American earnings story has a name and address: the physical supply chain of artificial intelligence, and within it, memory. Nine of the twelve largest contributors to the S&P 500 during the half-year were chip and memory companies, and Micron alone accounted for 16% of the index's gain, with a 304% rise over the period. SanDisk rose 858%, the largest gain in the entire S&P 500. Nvidia, already repriced in the prior cycle, rose 7%, and the Magnificent Seven fell 2%: leadership did not come from the GPU maker, but from memory suppliers, where earnings surprised this half-year. The market is no longer paying for the promise of AI. It is paying for the earnings of those who sell its infrastructure, and distrusting those who must turn the US$725 billion in annual capex from the four largest platforms into revenue. For now, every dollar invested in infrastructure generates about ten cents of AI revenue. The picks-and-shovels thesis won the half-year precisely because downstream monetization remains unproven.

Data center euphoria reached Brazil as an even longer-dated promise. The up to R$ 200 billion announced in the country promises cash further out than American capex, and without the present-tense earnings that carried semiconductors: the country operates less than 1 gigawatt, connection requests total 26 to 38 gigawatts, nearly 80% of curtailed power occurs precisely in the Northeast of these projects, and the sector's tax regime lapsed in February and awaits the Senate. If the market distrusted even American capex, which is at least backed by current revenue, the Brazilian bet is a decade-long one, not a 2026 catalyst. The same discount that rewarded memory's present earnings punishes any promise that distant.

Bitcoin Spent Six Months Refuting the Thesis That Made It Famous

If the half-year punished promise, Bitcoin was the most exposed promise of all.

The asset fell 33% in dollar terms, from US$87,700 to US$58,600, and closed June more than 50% below its October 2025 high. Ethereum lost 47%. US spot ETFs recorded their largest monthly outflows since launch. And Strategy, the market's largest leveraged Bitcoin vehicle, saw its multiple over net asset value fall below 1, made its first coin sale since 2022, and announced a program authorizing the monetization of up to US$1.25 billion in BTC. With the multiple below 1, every new share issued destroys Bitcoin per share instead of creating it, and Strategy's leveraged accumulation model reverses. What broke was the vehicle's engineering, not the asset's protocol, which keeps issuing at its same old algorithmic pace. Bitcoin's price fell on its own, under the weight of interest rates, not dragged down by Strategy.

The reading matters more than the numbers. The dominant thesis of the previous cycle was that of an uncorrelated store of value, digital gold. A half-year with war in the Middle East, oil above US$126, and pressured global inflation was the ideal scenario for that thesis to work. It didn't work. Bitcoin fell alongside long bonds and against the grain of AI stocks, behaving like the market's longest-duration asset, a cash flow promised for never. What this half-year failed was the uncorrelated store-of-value function, in the perfect scenario for it. The scarcity thesis and the network-adoption thesis were not at stake here: they are decided over years, not six months. A bad quarter brings down a hedge, it doesn't judge a protocol.

A narrative is circulating that capital migrated from crypto to AI stocks. I record it as a hypothesis, not as fact. The simultaneity is real, but calendar correlation doesn't prove flow. The more parsimonious explanation is a common factor, the liquidity squeeze from high real rates, which punishes the cashless speculative asset and rewards the asset with proven earnings.

The Local Investor Did the Exact Opposite of the Foreigner

This is the data point that retrospectives ignore, and it's the half-year's most revealing one.

Month by month, Brazilian institutional investors operated as a mirror image of foreign capital. While foreigners bought R$ 26.3 billion in January, locals sold R$ 17.5 billion. February, March, and April repeated the pattern. In May and June, when foreigners withdrew R$ 23.7 billion, locals bought back R$ 16.7 billion. Accumulated through the end of June: foreigners plus R$ 32.9 billion, local institutions minus R$ 33.0 billion. Locals sold the entire rally and bought the entire correction.

In realized terms, institutions got it right: they sold in the 185,000-to-190,000-point range, with selling concentrated near the April peak, and bought back near 172,000. But analytical honesty demands the caveat. Part of this selling wasn't conviction, it was cash to pay redemptions. Local equity funds lost R$ 11 billion in net inflows for the year, while international equity funds raised R$ 11.8 billion. Brazilian fund investors swapped the local stock market for the foreign one, the same risk at a different address, a move that high carry does not explain. Less allocation calculus, more fatigue with the market itself.

And the destination of domestic capital tells the rest of the story. Secondary-market turnover of incentivized debentures reached R$ 179.4 billion between January and May, a record for the period in the historical series, up 32.7% over 2025. The detail almost no one read: primary issuance fell 5.8% over the same span. This isn't the market financing more infrastructure. It's the same stock of tax-exempt paper churning faster and faster among investors competing for carry. Brazilian capital did not migrate toward risk. It migrated toward yield, and yield paid off.

The Half-Year This Text Describes Has Already Ended Differently

Every thesis needs boundaries, and this one has three.

The first is geopolitical. The half-year closed under a Middle East truce that pushed Brent down from US$126 to US$73. That truce collapsed on July 8, and the barrel returned to the US$85-to-90 range. America's June inflation relief, with the consumer price index falling 0.4% for the month, its largest monthly drop since 2020, is a child of cheap oil. The connection to the thesis is direct: cheap oil loosens inflation, lower inflation opens room for the Fed, and the Fed's room defines the global discount rate that ordered every price this half-year. A shock that reignites oil runs this chain in reverse and re-tightens the discount across every market at once.

The second is commercial. On July 16, the United States announced an additional 25% tariff on Brazilian goods, effective July 22. The exclusions for coffee, beef, orange juice, and aircraft reveal a design meant to minimize American domestic inflation. The direct economic impact on Brazil is sectoral and manageable, and that's not how it enters the thesis. A US tariff on Brazil does not move the global discount rate. It moves the local risk premium, through a political channel: in an election year, the tariff becomes fuel for the fight, and the fight is the largest generator of idiosyncratic risk premium on the Brazilian curve in the second half. It is through this domestic discount, not the global one, that it reaches prices.

The third bounds the central thesis itself. The argument that the Brazilian discount has no catalyst depends on inflation staying pressured. The most recent data point in the opposite direction: June's IPCA rose 0.16%, half the projected median of 0.31%, and the twelve-month accumulated figure slowed from 4.72% to 4.64%, while Focus still projects 5.30% for December. The yield curve prices in a deterioration that, for now, is not materializing. If the next readings confirm disinflation, the Copom, which cut Selic to 14.25% in June, gains room, the real rate gives way, and the stock market's discount finds exactly the trigger that was missing in the first half. Add the October election, historically the largest generator of idiosyncratic volatility on the Brazilian calendar, and the second half is far from a simple extrapolation of the first.

The Yardstick Decides the Verdict Before the Return

The first half of 2026 was not a contest between stocks and fixed income. It was a demonstration that, with real rates at this level, the discount rate governs every price. It chose the winners in the United States, manufactured and dismantled the Brazilian rally, crushed long bonds, and stripped Bitcoin bare.

The question of the yardstick remains. Saying the stock market lost to the CDI is true, and that's what this text did. But choosing the CDI as a universal benchmark is itself an analytical decision, not a law of nature. The paper Repensando o ativo sem risco, by Marinho, Ribeiro, and Wichmann, published in June, argues that the risk-free asset is a function of each investor's liabilities, and that for long horizons the inflation-linked bond fills that role better than the floating-rate one. By the CDI yardstick, whoever held long inflation-linked bonds lost badly during the half-year. By the yardstick of a long-term real liability, whoever held them locked in IPCA plus 7.5% for a decade, and may have made the best trade of the six months.

Return is a number. The verdict depends on the yardstick. And the yardstick, in Brazil, is almost never discussed. It's inherited.
