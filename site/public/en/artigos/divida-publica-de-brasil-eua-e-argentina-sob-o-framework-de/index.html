Public Debt of Brazil, the US, and Argentina Under Ray Dalio's Framework

Alisson Prata, CFP® · 4 de agosto de 2026

Application of the Chapter 3 framework from "How Countries Go Broke" (Ray Dalio, 2025) to Brazil, the United States, and Argentina, 2023 to 2025.

Why Look at Debt Over Revenue, Not Just Over GDP

Brazil's public debt equals about 430% of annual government revenue. The US figure is 578%. Argentina's, depending on the exchange rate used in the conversion, falls between 400% and over 1,000%. None of these numbers show up in the most cited indicator, debt over GDP, which is useful for historical comparison but measures the size of the economy, not the revenue that actually pays down the debt.

Dalio himself uses debt over GDP in the book's historical comparisons, like everyone else, for data convenience. But he is explicit that, for government solvency, the ratio that matters is debt over revenue. The reason is mechanical. The government doesn't pay debt with the economy's gross output, but with what it collects. A country with high GDP and a low tax burden has less debt-service capacity than the picture based on output suggests. Both metrics are approximations, but only one reflects the cash flow that honors the debt.

Swapping the denominator changes the reading. Over revenue, and on a comparable dollar basis, the three countries carry debts between 400% and 580% of annual revenue collection. The gap between them narrows, and what sets them apart stops being the size of the debt. It becomes the price each one pays to carry it.

The Equation That Determines Whether Debt Grows or Stabilizes

The framework rests on a simple identity. The debt-over-revenue ratio rises when the nominal interest rate exceeds the nominal growth of revenue and there is a primary deficit. It falls when the opposite happens. The variable Dalio calls the most important is precisely the gap between the nominal interest rate and the nominal growth of income.

From this follows a specific rate that stabilizes the debt. I call it the stabilizing i. It equals the nominal growth of revenue minus the ratio of the primary deficit to debt. If the country runs a primary deficit, the stabilizing rate sits below growth. If it runs a surplus, it rises above growth, because the fiscal effort buys room to pay higher interest without expanding the debt. When the effective interest rate runs above this rate, debt over revenue grows. When it runs below, it falls.

The indicator that sums it all up is the stabilization gap, the difference between the effective interest rate and the stabilizing rate. A positive gap means an upward trajectory. A negative gap means deleveraging. This is the yardstick I apply to the three geographies.

The yardstick is not my adaptation. The equation for the stabilizing rate is Dalio's, exactly as he writes it in Chapter 3, and the value I calculate for US debt over revenue, 578%, matches the 583% he reports in the book. This match doesn't validate the methodology itself; it validates that my implementation correctly reproduces the author's calculation. It's the minimum check before applying it to Brazil and Argentina, geographies the book doesn't cover.

There's also a distinction that separates the regimes. Debt in one's own currency falls into the deflationary category. The country can, as a last resort, print and devalue to dilute the stock, at an inflationary cost, but without a formal default. Debt in foreign currency falls into the inflationary category, and it isn't solved by issuance, because the currency that gets printed isn't the currency that is owed. Brazil holds about 95% of its debt in local currency, firmly in the first category. Argentina operates with a mixed debt, something like 45% in pesos and 55% in hard currency, and it's this mix that, as I'll show, distorts the entire reading of its indicators.

Three Countries, the Same Math, 2023 to 2025

I start with Brazil, because in it Dalio's mechanism played out in plain sight, over two consecutive years and in opposite directions.

It's a measured fact that general government gross debt went from R$ 8.1 trillion in 2023 to R$ 10.0 trillion in 2025, while central government net revenue went from R$ 1,899 billion to R$ 2,332 billion. The debt-over-revenue ratio went from 427% to 416% in 2024 and back up to 430% in 2025. The drop and the rebound aren't noise. They are the equation.

In 2024, the year-end Selic rate stood at 12.25% and revenue grew 13.85% in nominal terms. The interest rate below growth produced a negative gap, and debt over revenue fell. In 2025, the Selic rose to 15% while revenue growth slowed to 7.86%. The interest rate jumped to more than seven points above growth, the gap turned sharply positive, and the ratio rose again. The book describes the mechanism, and the 2024 and 2025 data behaved consistently with it. It isn't proof, it's an observation aligned with what the equation predicts.

For 2026, the stabilization math works out as follows. Under the assumption of nominal revenue growth of 10.46%, consistent with the first-bimester revenue and expenditure assessment report, and a projected primary deficit of R$ 59.8 billion over a debt of R$ 10.0 trillion, the deficit-over-debt ratio is 0.60%. The stabilizing rate comes out to 9.87%. The prevailing Selic rate is 14.25%. The gap is 4.4 percentage points, positive. Debt over revenue remains on an upward path.

The point that deserves attention isn't the number for a single year. It's the persistence. Extending the projection with the medians from the Focus report of July 24, 2026, the Selic rate recedes to 12% in 2027 and 10.5% in 2028. Even so, the gap remains positive across the entire horizon, something like 4.1, then 6.3, then 3.8 points. The reason is that the stabilizing rate requires an interest rate in the range of 6% to 7%, and the macroeconomic setup doesn't deliver enough nominal growth without reigniting inflation. Brazil's squeeze doesn't come from a lack of revenue. It comes from a real cost of debt structurally above the nominal growth the economy can sustain.

It's worth noting a divergence that the market itself makes explicit. The official scenario, from the budget guidelines bill, assumes a primary surplus starting in 2027. The Focus consensus doesn't believe it. It projects a primary deficit across the entire horizon, from 0.50% of GDP in 2026 to 0.25% in 2028, and net public sector debt rising from 69.8% to 76.4% of GDP. In other words, the market's reading reaches the same gap diagnosis through an independent path. The consensus doesn't see the primary adjustment that would zero out the math.

The United States: The Reserve-Currency Contrast

The United States offers the contrast that gives the comparison its meaning. It's a measured fact that debt held by the public reached US$ 30.3 trillion in fiscal year 2025, against revenue of US$ 5.2 trillion. The debt-over-revenue ratio is 578%, the highest of the trio. And yet, the effective rate on that debt is only 3.2%, the result of US$ 970 billion in net interest on the stock.

Here, Dalio himself supplies the number, and it rules out any comforting reading. The nominal growth projected by the Congressional Budget Office is 3.9%, made up of 1.9% real growth and 2% inflation, not the 5% or 6% a hasty reading would assume. With that growth and a primary deficit of around 12% of revenue, the rate that would stabilize US debt is about 1%. The rate the Treasury pays today runs at 3.2% effective on the stock, and about 4.5% on the ten-year yield that anchors new issuance. The gap is adverse by two to three percentage points. This isn't debt at the edge of balance. It's debt that is already growing, and growing at a cost that would be prohibitive in any other country. By Dalio's own math, stabilizing it would require cutting the interest rate to 1%, or accepting 4.5% inflation, or cutting spending by 12%, or raising revenue by 11%. These percentages are measured over government spending and revenue, not GDP, and they're only enough to stabilize the ten-year trajectory, not to reduce the debt. None of these paths is politically trivial. The US debt-over-revenue ratio, currently 583% by the book's calculation, rises to 648% by 2035 under the Congressional Budget Office's own scenario.

The Cost of Carrying the Debt, Not Just Its Size

I place side by side the 2025 snapshot, with the four core indicators Dalio uses to judge sustainability.

The line that matters is the first one, but the second is the one that bites. The United States carries the largest debt-over-revenue ratio in the group, 578%, at the lowest cost, 3.2%. Brazil carries a proportionally smaller debt, 430%, at the highest cost among the economies with their own currency, 14.25%. An eleven-percentage-point gap in carrying cost, with relatively larger debt on the American side, isn't explained by comparative fiscal discipline. It reflects the reserve-currency premium, expressed in operational terms. The world finances the US Treasury at a cost no other issuer gets, and that shows up directly when you place cost alongside the stock, not in the debt-over-GDP ranking.

Reading the stock alone understates how much this weighs on cash. Debt service consumes about 43% of all Brazilian government revenue, against 18% of US revenue. Brazil spends more than double the share of its revenue collection just to roll over interest, despite owing proportionally less. It's the same reserve-currency premium, now expressed in what's left over for the rest of the budget after paying the creditor. Argentina, on paper, spends only 7% of revenue on cash interest, but the figure is deceiving: most of the cost of its securities capitalizes into the stock itself rather than being paid, which artificially lowers the apparent debt service while the debt grows from within.

There's a maturity premium hidden inside the reserve-currency premium. Brazilian debt is mostly indexed to the Selic rate or short-term, so a high interest rate hits the stock almost immediately. American debt has long maturities and fixed coupons, and only absorbs a rate hike as bonds mature and roll over, about a third of the stock per year. The United States absorbs the tightening slowly, Brazil almost immediately, and Argentina, at the extreme, rolls over bills of just a few months, so that any interest-rate shock passes through to cost almost instantly. The same nominal rate weighs differently depending on the speed at which the debt reprices, and that speed is part of what Dalio observes when he analyzes maturity structure.

The reserve-currency premium is the variable that dissolves the paradox of the United States living with the highest leverage and the lowest short-term tension. It doesn't show up in the debt-over-GDP ranking. It shows up when you ask how much the debt costs, not how much it weighs.

What the 2025 Snapshot Doesn't Show

The reserve-currency premium isn't a permanent attribute. It's a variable, and the United States itself is eroding it. Dalio is direct about the mechanism: governments that hold a reserve currency tend to abuse the privilege, borrowing more than enough to lose it, which makes the decline sharper when it comes. Today's adverse gap isn't a passing anomaly. It coexists with primary deficits the Congressional Budget Office projects at US$ 2.9 trillion through 2034. The question isn't whether the privilege exists, but for how long the market keeps renewing it under current conditions. The premium buys time, but doesn't by itself resolve the solvency trajectory.

Argentina: The Metric Under the Exchange Rate

Argentina hides something different and more uncomfortable. In it, the metric rebels against the analyst, because the problem lies in the yardstick itself, not just in what it measures.

I redenominated the Argentine series in pesos, following Dalio's rule of measuring debt in the currency of denomination. The result revealed that Argentina's debt-over-revenue ratio isn't a single number. It's a range, and the width of that range depends entirely on which exchange rate is used to convert the debt.

On a dollar basis, converted at the year's average exchange rate, the ratio orbits around 400% throughout the period. On a peso basis, converted at the closing exchange rate, it explodes and comes to exceed 1,000% in 2023. The table below shows both series. The gap between them is brutal, and it isn't fiscal. The explanation is the December 2023 maxi-devaluation, which pushed the year-end debt to an exchange rate of 808 pesos per dollar while the year's GDP had been measured at an implicit average exchange rate of about 296. The jump is an artifact of the moment at which the exchange rate is snapshotted.

There's a sanity check that points to which basis is more consistent. Argentina's debt-over-GDP ratio on a peso basis, about 78% at the end of 2025, matches what official sources and market aggregators report for the country, while conversion at the average dollar exchange rate produces a lower figure, near 67%. To compare each country in its own currency, as is done with Brazil and the United States, the peso basis is the one that fits the standard. The dollar reading tends to understate, which is why I treat both bases side by side instead of picking whichever is more convenient.

The trajectory of the three geographies on the same scale makes the difference in nature evident. Brazil and the United States swing a few points a year. Argentina swings by hundreds, and the amplitude depends on which exchange rate is chosen.

This sheds light on an episode that, without the decomposition, would be misread. In 2024, Argentina carried out the largest fiscal adjustment of the trio, from a primary deficit of 2.9% of GDP to a surplus of 1.8%. And yet its debt-over-revenue ratio, measured in dollars, worsened from 390% to 470%. The cause wasn't spending. It was peso-denominated debt appreciating in dollar terms after exchange-rate stabilization, while dollar GDP shrank. Dalio would identify this as the mistake of measuring debt in the wrong currency. The adjustment happened. The indicator hid it.

I need to be explicit about a limit of my own construction, because it conditions how much can be claimed. For 2023 and 2024, I don't have Argentine revenue measured independently, and I assume it represented 16% of GDP, consistent with the only measured year, 2025, when it came in near 16%. Under that assumption, Argentina's debt-over-revenue ratio is just the debt-over-GDP ratio multiplied by a constant. This text's central innovation, measuring over revenue rather than GDP, gets neutralized precisely in Argentina's case. For Brazil and the United States, revenue is measured year by year and the distinction is informative. For Argentina, it adds nothing, and it's honest to say so before the sophisticated reader notices on their own.

With this choice of basis made, Argentina's stabilization gap is calculable, and in 2025 it was favorable. With nominal national accounts growth of about 45%, the rate that would stabilize the debt comes in near 47%, above the roughly 40% the debt effectively pays. The resulting gap is approximately negative seven points, and the observed data confirms the direction: debt over revenue on a peso basis fell from 526% to 489% over the year. If nominal growth had been the 34% of end-to-end inflation, the debt would have risen, and it fell instead, which rules out that reading.

The nature of this deleveraging matters more than its sign. It comes from inflation, not growth. Most of the 45% nominal growth is price repricing, not productive expansion, and inflation erodes the peso debt stock faster than interest rebuilds it. Dalio would call this inflationary deleveraging, not beautiful deleveraging. It's favorable and fragile at the same time, because it unravels if disinflation knocks down nominal growth or if a new exchange-rate jump reprices the dollar-linked share of the debt.

There's also the capitalization of the LECAPs, capitalizing bills, which added up to about 10 trillion pesos in 2024 and 2025 without passing through the cash result. It doesn't change the debt-over-revenue ratio, because it's already in the stock. But it contaminates the reading of the primary surplus. A formal primary surplus doesn't mean the debt is being paid down, when the central bank's remunerated cost is absorbed by interest that capitalizes into the stock itself. A green primary balance and growing debt can coexist, and in Argentina's case, they do.

Where the Math Doesn't Close

None of these readings is a clean snapshot, and acknowledging the limits is part of the rigor.

The Argentine dollar series is distorted by the exchange rate, and the peso series is distorted by a mismatch between the year-end stock and the revenue flow measured in average-value pesos, which are cheaper under 45% inflation. The two bases err in opposite directions, which is why I present both instead of pretending precision in one. The Brazilian projections beyond 2026 depend on four layers of assumption, spanning Focus, the budget, and the fiscal framework, and they stand as an order of magnitude, not as a precise point. Brazil's projected debt-over-revenue level, something like 491% in 2028, is a repetition of the official projection, not an independent derivation. What I hold with conviction in this projection is the gap, which follows from observable growth and primary balance, not the exact level of the stock. And the 2025 American stability is sensitive to less than one point of nominal growth, which already shifts it to slightly adverse.

There's also a scope limit, prior to the data. Dalio's framework isolates a specific mechanism, the dynamic between interest, growth, and primary balance over the debt stock. It doesn't measure productivity, demographics, institutional quality, reform capacity, or political stability, factors that also determine a country's solvency over the long run. An adverse stabilization gap signals pressure on the debt trajectory, not a destiny. What I'm comparing here is that mechanics, not the complete economic health of the three geographies.

I state these limitations not to weaken the argument, but because an argument that survives the disclosure of its premises is stronger than one that depends on hiding them.

What Dalio's Yardstick Reveals

Brazil, the United States, and Argentina face the same mechanism, with radically different intensities and instruments. The interest rate that stabilizes the debt is calculable for each of them. And none of the three is operating with room below it in the 2025 base scenario.

Brazil runs with a gap of more than four points, and the market consensus doesn't see the primary adjustment that would close it. The United States already runs with an adverse gap, of two to three points, shielded by a reserve-currency premium that it is itself eroding, with the budget curve pointing upward. Argentina had a favorable gap in 2025, the only one of the trio, but through inflationary deleveraging, not growth, and in a regime where the reading of debt still depends heavily on the exchange rate.

Dalio gives a name to what comes next, when the gap doesn't close. If the cost of debt rises because the market judges it unsustainable, the perception feeds on itself. Higher interest raises credit risk, which reduces demand for the bonds, which pushes the interest rate even higher. This is what Dalio calls the debt death spiral. The ways out are known, and all are costly: austerity, which contracts the economy because one person's spending is another's income; default or restructuring, which is deflationary because one person's debt is another's asset; central bank issuance, which is inflationary and devalues the currency; or tax increases. None of these ways out is painless, and the choice comes down to which cost to bear. It's worth noting that Brazil and the United States are cited in the book itself as cases where social spending has become too large to cut without political controversy.

The conclusion isn't about which country is worse off. It's about what this lens adds. When GDP is complemented with revenue, and the question becomes not just how much is owed, but at what price what is owed gets carried, the three cases stop looking like separate risk categories and become distinct points on the same equation. And that equation, unlike the debt-over-GDP ranking, allows projecting the path ahead, not just describing the present.
