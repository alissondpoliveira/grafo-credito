From Global Inflation to the Client's Portfolio: The Chain That Ran Through Expert XP 2026

Alisson Prata, CFP® · 29 de julho de 2026

The 16th edition of Expert XP took place between July 23 and 25 at São Paulo Expo, with 350 speakers, more than 120 hours of programming, and 64,000 attendees, according to the organizers. The edition marked XP Inc.'s 25th anniversary.

Panels with Janet Yellen, Gabriel Galípolo, Niall Ferguson, and an anthropologist specializing in high-net-worth individuals occupied different rooms and addressed topics that do not overlap. Seen together, they cover distinct links in the same causal chain, one that connects the global supply shock to an allocation decision made by a client in the interior of Minas Gerais.

Supply shocks and artificial intelligence set the floor for the global cost of capital

Janet Yellen, former US Treasury Secretary, opened the first day with Caio Megale, XP's chief economist. She described an environment of successive supply shocks that keep US inflation above the 2% target over a five-year horizon. On rates, she said the Fed is in a holding pattern, but ready to raise rates if tension in the Middle East escalates.

The counterintuitive part came on artificial intelligence. According to Yellen, AI is currently raising inflation, not reducing it, because there is still no evidence of an aggregate productivity gain that would justify a change in monetary policy. The parallel she drew was with the late 1990s, when the Federal Reserve bet on productivity gains from the internet. The difference is that in that cycle the gain showed up in the data; in this one, it has not shown up yet, while investment in AI infrastructure is already pressuring demand for energy, equipment, and skilled labor.

On the dollar, Yellen acknowledged gradual erosion of its share in global reserves, from more than 70% to just above 50%, but maintained that there is no alternative market with the same depth, liquidity, and institutional backing.

Niall Ferguson closed the first day with Artur Wichmann, adding the geopolitical vector to the same mechanism. He framed the dispute between the United States and China as the defining relationship of the coming decades and pointed to the Taiwan Strait, not the Strait of Hormuz, as the world's most dangerous problem today, due to the concentration of global semiconductor production there. Fragmentation of the supply chain is a permanent supply shock, and a permanent supply shock is structural inflation, which means high rates.

In the CEO panel, moderated by Rafael Furlanetti, the same theme appeared from the real economy side. Gilberto Tomazoni, of JBS, explained the preference for acquisition over building from scratch based on prior knowledge of the asset. Paulo Moll described Rede D'Or using AI to capture information from medical records and support diagnosis. André Bier Gerdau Johannpeter addressed longevity as adaptive capacity and argued for separating the board from operations in succession planning. And Ederson Muffato gave the event's most unexpected piece of information by identifying online betting as the main competitor to food retail, since both compete for the same household income.

In Brazil, fiscal imbalance turned high rates into a prolonged condition

The second day moved down from the global scenario to the domestic mechanism, and the transition was direct: if the global cost of capital is high, the Brazilian cost of capital is even higher, and the reason is fiscal.

Gabriel de Barros, of ARX, estimated the need for structural adjustment starting in 2027 of around 4% of a GDP projected at R$ 13 trillion, something close to R$ 500 billion, with gross debt approaching 85% of GDP. Bruno Funchal, of Bradesco Asset and a former Treasury official, added the institutional point: a written fiscal rule does not produce credibility on its own; there must be a genuine willingness to spend below the ceiling for the effect to materialize. Dario Durigan, executive secretary of the Finance Ministry, presented the government's reading, attributing the year's inflationary pressure to the Iran war, rejecting the thesis of election-year fiscal expansion, and citing a primary adjustment equivalent to about 2% of GDP.

Gabriel Galípolo, in the monetary policy panel, addressed the consequences of this for the Selic rate and took the opportunity to respond to a recurring accusation with data, not rhetoric. The thesis that the Central Bank calibrates rates by listening only to the financial market, which would have an incentive to project high IPCA, is testable. Since the fourth quarter of 2023 the Central Bank has run the Firmus Survey, which asks non-financial sector companies about the same variables as the Focus Bulletin, created in 1999. If the accusation held, the productive sector's expectations would systematically come in lower than the market's. According to Galípolo, throughout the series they have come in equal to or higher than the market's.

What the two surveys show unambiguously is that de-anchoring is not a phenomenon restricted to the financial market. In the Firmus survey released in June 2026, with 349 companies surveyed in May, the median IPCA for 2026 rose from 4% to 5%. The Focus Bulletin from July 20 showed 5.15%. The two figures are not directly comparable, since they were collected two months apart and in that interval there was an escalation in the Middle East and an oil shock. But both point in the same direction, with the productive sector and the financial market projecting IPCA well above the 3% target.

Galípolo described the Central Bank as moving like an ocean liner, not a jet ski, defended restrictive rates for a longer period, and avoided signaling the next move on the Selic. Among the long-term pressure factors, he listed oil, El Niño, and investment in artificial intelligence. It's worth noting the convergence with Yellen the day before: two central banks, in different economies, treating AI as a short-term inflationary factor, not a disinflationary one. Convergence between monetary authorities is a shared reading, not proof. Both are observing the same channel, infrastructure investment pressuring demand for energy, equipment, and labor, and there is a counter-argument that cost deflation in technology-intensive services is already underway and would show up in the data with a lag. What both affirm is that this second leg has not yet appeared with enough magnitude to change the monetary policy decision.

High and prolonged rates change the definition of what protects wealth

This is where the chain reaches the portfolio, and this is where the event produced its most technical contribution.

Artur Wichmann and Ruy Ribeiro presented the study, also signed with Eduardo Marinho. The structural argument: the CDI offers predictability over a horizon of approximately 45 days, the interval between Copom meetings. For longer terms, the investor carries reinvestment risk and unanticipated inflation risk. During the inflationary shock of 2020 and 2021, the CDI's real return was negative, with the investor losing purchasing power while the statement showed a growing balance.

Ribeiro proposed the conceptual inversion: the risk-free asset is the one that protects against not reaching the goal, not the one that swings less tomorrow. And he showed that an NTN-B held for two years shows lower purchasing-power volatility than the CDI over the same period, which runs counter to the intuition of anyone looking at daily mark-to-market.

The inversion has a limit that needs to be made explicit, because the panel addressed horizon, not liquidity. The NTN-B protects purchasing power when held to maturity. An unplanned need to redeem along the way exposes the investor to mark-to-market, and the nominal loss in that scenario can be significant. The CDI carries reinvestment risk and inflation risk, and carries no nominal capital risk or liquidity risk. These are risks of different natures, not different degrees of the same risk. That's why the CDI remains the right instrument for an emergency fund, transactional cash, and any short-term or undetermined-term liability. The mistake the panel describes is not using the CDI; it's using it as a benchmark for long-term wealth protection.

The connection to the previous link is direct. The CDI's implicit promise for long terms is the stability of the rate that defines it. That rate is set by a monetary authority that, as the fiscal and monetary panels on the same day made clear, operates under fiscal constraints and works with scenarios of pause and resumption. Betting on long-term predictability of a very-short-term instrument is betting on a stability that the previous panels themselves classified as uncertain.

The day before, Rodrigo Sgavioli had already formulated the question this panel answered, observing that anyone investing in CDI doesn't know what the return will be in ten years.

Two private credit panels showed the other side of the same movement. Delano Macêdo, of Solis, addressed retail access to senior FIDC shares, made possible by CVM Resolution 175, in effect since October 2023, betting on a fund-of-funds structure instead of direct purchase. And Petrônio Cançado, of Occam, alongside Pierre Massari Jadoul, of ARX, and Leonardo Ono, of Legacy, raised the question of the sustainability of the tax exemption on incentivized debentures under Law 12,431, pointing to the practical problem of investors buying expecting traditional fixed-income behavior and instead finding long maturities, IPCA indexation, and mark-to-market volatility. It's the same CDI issue seen in the mirror: the right instrument, the wrong horizon in the mind of the buyer.

Thiago Maffra had established the commercial implication of this on the first day, in the panel on XP's 25th anniversary, by saying that the next frontier of advisory services is selling a solution, not a standalone product, incorporating tax planning, estate planning, and insurance into the scope.

The final link is behavioral, and it's where correct strategies tend to fail

A technically correct allocation to long real rates produces mark-to-market swings. This means the right strategy delivers, in the short term, a worse experience than the wrong strategy. The last link in the chain is what determines whether the client stays.

Michel Alcoforado, an anthropologist with fifteen years of research on high-net-worth individuals in Brazil, described the pattern of managing one's lifestyle always one notch above income, which increases debt and reduces investing capacity. He also noted that family wealth in Brazil typically lasts five generations, and that breaking that cycle depends on the family understanding that it needs to hand down to the next generation at least what it received.

Andre Agassi, in conversation with José Berenguer, described the turning point of 1997, when he fell to 141st in the ranking and then returned to the top: the difference was shifting focus to the next point instead of reacting to scoreboards. Rodrigo Nogueira, "Minotauro," in the retirement planning panel moderated by Luiza Valdetaro and Rodrigo Saldanha, addressed the distinction between achieving success and preserving it, and the need to turn to specialists after early-career mistakes.

At the event's close, João Fonseca, Lucas Pinheiro Braathen, and Caio Amato, Oakley's global president, addressed the same theme from the perspective of elite athletic performance. Fonseca described pressure as a privilege to be channeled and said he doesn't want to be the next Guga, but rather to build his own story.

The complete chain

Global supply shock and geopolitical fragmentation sustain structural inflation. Structural inflation sustains high rates. In Brazil, fiscal imbalance prolongs high rates beyond what activity would justify. High and prolonged rates change the operational definition of what counts as a safe asset, shifting the axis from nominal floating-rate instruments to long-term real rates. And long-term real-rate assets swing, which transfers the problem from the technical field to the behavioral one.

No single panel at Expert XP 2026 contained this entire chain. Each one contained a link, with its own data and its own expert. For those who work with asset allocation, the event's value wasn't in any specific panel — it was in seeing the links fit together.
